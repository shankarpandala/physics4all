Semiconductors

Doping, junctions, transistors — band theory becomes civilization

Electrons, Holes, and the Gap

A semiconductor is an insulator with a small enough gap that room temperature matters: silicon's is 1.12 eV, germanium's 0.67 eV, gallium arsenide's 1.42 eV. Thermal agitation promotes a tiny fraction of valence electrons across the gap, and each promotion creates two mobile objects: the electron now in the conduction band, and the vacancy it left behind.

That vacancy deserves its own name. A nearly full valence band with one state empty responds to fields exactly as if it contained a single positive particle — the hole — with charge +e and its own effective mass. Holes are real quasiparticles: they drift, diffuse, carry current, and annihilate with electrons (releasing the gap energy — remember that for the LED section). Currents in semiconductors are always a two-carrier story.

The intrinsic carrier density follows Boltzmann statistics across the gap:

ni ∝ e−Eg/2kBT

For silicon at 300 K, ni ≈ 10¹⁶ per m³ — roughly one carrier per 10¹³ atoms. Compare copper's one per atom: pure silicon is a terrible conductor. But the exponential makes it exquisitely sensitive: silicon's carrier density roughly doubles every 8 °C, so semiconductor conductivity rises with temperature, the exact opposite of metals — a clean experimental fingerprint of the gap, and the principle behind thermistor thermometers.

Analogy: The Packed Theater

The valence band is a packed theater row: nobody can move. Promote one person to the empty balcony (conduction band) and two things become mobile — the person upstairs, free to wander, and the empty seat below, which drifts along the row as neighbors shuffle into it. Watching the row from afar, you see a "bubble" moving opposite to the shufflers: that is a hole.

Did you know?

Pure enough silicon barely existed before the 1950s. Modern semiconductor-grade silicon is refined to about one foreign atom per 10 billion — the purest material humans mass-produce — precisely so that the only impurities present are the ones engineers add on purpose. Why they add them is the next section.

Doping: One Atom in a Million Changes Everything

Intrinsic silicon is feeble; the magic is that its carrier population can be dictated at will. Replace a silicon atom (four valence electrons) with phosphorus (five): four electrons slot into the bonds, and the fifth is left orbiting the P⁺ core in a hydrogen-like orbit — but a hydrogen atom drastically weakened, because silicon's dielectric constant screens the Coulomb attraction and the electron's effective mass is small. Its binding energy is only ~45 meV, so at room temperature it ionizes: the electron joins the conduction band. Phosphorus is a donor, and the material is n-type — conduction dominated by negative carriers.

Replace silicon with boron (three valence electrons) instead and one bond is left starving; it grabs an electron from a neighbor, launching a mobile hole into the valence band. Boron is an acceptor; the material is p-type.

The leverage is staggering. Doping at one part per million puts ~5 × 10²² donors per m³ into silicon — several million times the intrinsic carrier density. A trace of deliberate dirt raises conductivity by factors of 10⁶ and, just as importantly, chooses the carrier's sign.

The law of mass action

In equilibrium the electron and hole populations balance like a chemical reaction: n·p = ni², independent of doping. Flooding silicon with donors raises n and suppresses p (recombination eats the minority holes). Every doped semiconductor thus has majority and minority carriers — and devices, it turns out, run on the minority.

All of microelectronics is patterned doping: n here, p there, drawn with photolithographic masks and implanted with ion beams at densities controlled to a percent. A modern chip is a single silicon crystal whose dopant map encodes billions of components. What happens at the border where n meets p is the next section — the most important interface in technology.

Did you know?

The donor electron's weakened "hydrogen atom" has a Bohr radius of about 3 nanometers — spanning roughly ten thousand silicon atoms. Subject 26's hydrogen solution, rescaled by ε and m*, predicts donor binding energies within tens of percent. Old quantum homework, new trillion-dollar industry.

The pn Junction: A One-Way Valve Built from Doping

Dope one half of a silicon crystal n-type and the other p-type, and the border does something remarkable all by itself. Electrons, abundant on the n side, diffuse across into the p side and annihilate with holes; holes diffuse the opposite way. Each departure leaves behind an ionized dopant frozen in the lattice — positive donors on the n side, negative acceptors on the p side. A charged double layer builds up, creating an electric field pointing from n to p across a thin depletion region (~1 μm, essentially emptied of mobile carriers).

The field opposes further diffusion, and equilibrium settles where drift cancels diffusion, leaving a built-in potential of about 0.7 V across the junction. In band language, the bands bend: carriers face a built-in energy hill.

Now apply a voltage, and the junction reveals its personality:

  • Forward bias (p side positive): the external voltage lowers the hill. Diffusion currents grow exponentially — every kBT/e ≈ 25 mV of forward voltage multiplies the current by e. The device conducts generously above ~0.6–0.7 V.
  • Reverse bias (p side negative): the hill grows, diffusion chokes off, and only a tiny leakage current of thermally generated minority carriers flows — microamps or less, nearly voltage-independent.

The result is the diode: current flows one way, essentially not the other, following I = I₀(eeV/kBT − 1). It is the first circuit element in history whose behavior is fundamentally asymmetric — a valve with no moving parts, built from nothing but a gradient of impurities.

Why this junction matters so much

Rectification alone (AC → DC conversion) would justify the diode's fame. But the pn junction is also the load-bearing wall of the transistor, the light source in an LED, and the charge separator in a solar cell — the next two sections are all variations on this single interface. Master the depletion region and you hold the master key to semiconductor devices.

Did you know?

The junction's exponential current law is so reliable that it doubles as a thermometer: the ~2 mV/°C drift of a diode's forward voltage is how your phone and laptop measure their own chip temperatures.

Transistors: The Switch That Built the World

A diode decides; a transistor decides on command — it is a valve for electrons controlled by electrons, and it is the most manufactured object in human history. The dominant design, the MOSFET (metal–oxide–semiconductor field-effect transistor), is band-bending in action.

Take p-type silicon with two n-type wells implanted in it — the source and drain. Between them lie two back-to-back pn junctions, so no current can pass: the switch is off. Above the gap sits the gate: a conducting electrode separated from the silicon by a nanometers-thin insulating oxide — a capacitor plate hovering over the channel. Apply a positive gate voltage and its field repels holes and pulls electrons to the surface, inverting a thin layer of the p-type silicon into effectively n-type. Suddenly an n-channel bridges source to drain and current flows: the switch is on. The gate draws essentially no steady current — it is a capacitor — so a tiny charge controls a large current: amplification and switching from pure electrostatics.

Wire two complementary MOSFETs together (CMOS) and you get logic gates that consume power only while switching; etch billions onto one crystal and you get a processor. The scaling story is famous: from centimeter-scale transistors in 1947 to features a few nanometers wide today, doubling in density every couple of years for five decades — Moore's law, physics' greatest gift to economics.

Did you know?

Humanity now fabricates on the order of 10²¹ transistors per year — hundreds of billions per person, more objects than grains of sand on Earth's beaches, each one a working quantum device. The 1956 Nobel Prize to Bardeen, Brattain, and Shockley may be the most consequential in the prize's history; Bardeen will appear again, uniquely, in this course's final chapter.

Where the physics you learned shows up

Every item on the transistor engineer's worry list is a chapter of this subject: gap and effective mass (band structure), carrier densities (doping statistics), junction fields (depletion physics), resistance (phonon and impurity scattering), and now quantum tunneling through the atomically thin gate oxide — a Subject 26 phenomenon that sets a hard floor on further miniaturization. Light In, Light Out: LEDs and Solar Cells

A pn junction converts between electrical and optical energy in both directions, and the two directions are reshaping the world's energy budget from opposite ends.

LED: current in, light out. Forward-bias a junction and electrons pour into the p side, where they recombine with holes — each recombination releasing the gap energy Eg. In the right material that energy exits as a photon with ħω ≈ Eg: the gap chooses the color. Infrared from GaAs (1.4 eV), red through green from GaP-based alloys, blue from GaN (3.4 eV). The catch is quantum bookkeeping: recombination must conserve crystal momentum. In direct-gap materials (GaAs, GaN) the band extrema align in k-space and photon emission is fast and efficient. Silicon's gap is indirect — the transition needs a phonon to balance momentum, is therefore slow, and the energy mostly degrades to heat. The material that owns electronics is nearly useless for light: hence the entire III–V compound industry.

Solar cell: light in, current out. Run the film backwards. A photon with ħω > Eg absorbed near the junction creates an electron–hole pair; the depletion region's built-in field tears the pair apart before it can recombine, sweeping electrons to the n side and holes to the p side. The junction becomes a pump driving current through an external circuit — sunlight to electricity with no moving parts. Single-junction cells face the Shockley–Queisser ceiling of ~33% (photons below the gap are wasted entirely; energy above the gap is shed as heat), which stacked multi-gap junctions push past 45%.

Did you know?

The blue LED earned the 2014 Nobel Prize: GaN resisted three decades of effort before Akasaki, Amano, and Nakamura tamed its growth and doping. Blue completed the palette for white light — and LED lighting now saves more electricity worldwide than many countries generate.

The chapter in one sentence

Band gaps store and release energy quanta; doping decides who carries charge; junction fields separate and direct them — and from those three principles follow the diode, the transistor, the LED, and the solar cell.
