Bonding & Crystal Structure

Lattices, the reciprocal lattice, and X-ray diffraction

Why Atoms Stick: The Bonding Zoo

Before asking how atoms arrange themselves, ask why they condense at all. Every solid is held together by electromagnetism plus quantum mechanics, but the compromise between electron kinetic energy, Coulomb attraction, and the Pauli principle plays out in several distinct styles — and the style predicts the material's personality.

  • Ionic bonding (NaCl): one atom donates an electron, the other accepts, and the resulting + and − ions attract. Strong, non-directional → hard, brittle, transparent crystals with high melting points that dissolve in water and conduct only when molten.
  • Covalent bonding (diamond, silicon): neighboring atoms share electrons in overlapping orbitals. Strongly directional — carbon insists on its tetrahedral 109.5° angles — giving rigid, hard crystals and, crucially for this subject, semiconductors.
  • Metallic bonding (Cu, Na, Fe): outer electrons delocalize completely, forming a negatively charged sea that glues the positive ion cores together. The sea explains everything metallic: electrical and thermal conduction, shininess, and ductility — ions can slide past each other without breaking directed bonds.
  • Van der Waals bonding (solid argon, the layers of graphite): fluctuating dipoles attract weakly. Soft solids, low melting points.
  • Hydrogen bonding (ice): a hydrogen shared between electronegative atoms — the reason ice floats and proteins fold.

Bonding is a competition

Squeezing electrons into small spaces costs kinetic energy (the uncertainty principle from Subject 26); letting them spread near several nuclei saves it. Solids form because some arrangement of nuclei lets the electrons strike a better energy bargain than isolated atoms could — typically about 1–10 eV per atom, the cohesive energy.

And why do the atoms then line up periodically? Because whatever local arrangement minimizes the energy for one atom's neighborhood minimizes it for every identical atom — so the optimal pattern repeats. Periodicity is not an assumption but an outcome, and it is spectacularly consequential: nearly everything in the next five chapters (diffraction, phonons, Bloch's theorem, band gaps) flows from translational symmetry alone.

Did you know?

Graphite and diamond are both pure carbon. Strong covalent sheets bound by feeble van der Waals forces make graphite soft enough for pencils; the same bonds arranged in a 3D tetrahedral network make diamond the hardest natural material. Structure, not chemistry, is destiny.

The Lattice Language

Crystallography's first move is to separate where things repeat from what repeats. A Bravais lattice is an infinite array of points generated by all integer combinations R = n₁a₁ + n₂a₂ + n₃a₃ of three primitive lattice vectors — an array that looks exactly the same viewed from any of its points. Attach to every lattice point an identical group of atoms, the basis, and you have a crystal:

crystal structure = lattice + basis

Analogy: Wallpaper

Wallpaper is a motif (a flower, say) stamped at every point of a repeating grid. The grid is the lattice; the flower is the basis. Two wallpapers can share the same grid with different motifs, or the same motif on different grids — and crystals work exactly the same way.

The volume that tiles space when repeated is a unit cell. A primitive cell contains exactly one lattice point; a conventional cell may contain more but shows the symmetry better. The three cubic lattices dominate this course:

  • Simple cubic (sc): points at cube corners only. Coordination number 6. Rare in nature (polonium is the lonely example).
  • Body-centered cubic (bcc): corners plus cube center. Coordination 8. Iron, chromium, tungsten, the alkali metals.
  • Face-centered cubic (fcc): corners plus face centers. Coordination 12, packing fraction 74% — the densest possible packing of spheres. Copper, aluminum, gold, silver.

The basis idea earns its keep immediately. Rock salt is an fcc lattice with a two-atom basis: Na at (0,0,0) and Cl at the point half a cube edge away. Diamond (and silicon) is an fcc lattice with a two-carbon basis — one lattice, two interpenetrating atomic families. Directions and planes get labels too: Miller indices (hkl) tag each family of parallel lattice planes, and the spacing between (hkl) planes is what X-rays will measure in the last section of this chapter.

Did you know?

Kepler conjectured in 1611 that fcc's 74% is the best any sphere packing can do — grocers stacking oranges agree. A rigorous proof took until 1998, and the referees needed four more years to check it.

The Reciprocal Lattice: The Crystal in Fourier Space

Here is the idea that unlocks the entire subject. Everything physical inside a crystal — electron density, potential energy — is periodic: f(r + R) = f(r) for every lattice vector R. Subject 23 taught that periodic functions are sums of plane waves, but only special plane waves fit a given periodicity. A wave eiG·r respects the lattice only if

eiG·R = 1, i.e. G·R = 2π × integer, for every lattice vector R.

The set of all wavevectors G that pass this test is itself a lattice — in k-space — called the reciprocal lattice. It is, quite literally, the Fourier transform of the crystal: the direct lattice says where atoms sit; the reciprocal lattice says which waves know about it. Its primitive vectors b₁, b₂, b₃ obey aᵢ·bⱼ = 2πδᵢⱼ, so big real-space spacings mean small reciprocal-space spacings and vice versa — hence "reciprocal."

Planes ↔ points

Each reciprocal lattice vector G is perpendicular to a family of Miller-index lattice planes, and the shortest such G has magnitude |G| = 2π/d, where d is the spacing of those planes. One point in reciprocal space encodes an entire stack of planes in real space. This dictionary is exactly what diffraction reads out.

Two facts to file away. First, a pleasing duality: the reciprocal lattice of fcc is bcc, and vice versa (sc is its own reciprocal). Second, the Brillouin zone: the region of k-space closer to G = 0 than to any other reciprocal lattice point — the Wigner–Seitz cell of the reciprocal lattice. Because adding any G to a wavevector changes nothing physical about a lattice wave, all distinct wave physics in a crystal lives inside this one zone. Phonon dispersions (next chapter) and electron band structures (chapter three) are always plotted across it.

Did you know?

Experimentalists live so completely in reciprocal space that a diffraction pattern feels more "real" to them than the atomic positions. A crystal's symmetry, orientation, and quality can all be judged at a glance in k-space — real space is what you reconstruct afterwards, by inverse Fourier transform.

X-ray Diffraction: Reading Atoms with Waves

To see structure of size d you need waves with λ ≈ d. Atomic spacings are around 0.2 nm — a thousand times smaller than visible light's wavelength, which is why no optical microscope will ever image a lattice. X-rays (and also neutrons and electrons of the right energy) have exactly the right λ, and in 1912–13 they turned crystallography from guesswork into measurement.

The Braggs' picture: treat each family of lattice planes, spacing d, as partially reflecting mirrors. Rays reflecting off adjacent planes travel an extra path 2d sin θ, and constructive interference requires

2d sin θ = nλ  (Bragg's law)

Sweep angles, record where sharp reflections blaze out, and read off every plane spacing in the crystal. Von Laue's equivalent, deeper statement uses the last section directly: an incoming wave k scatters into k′ with constructive interference only when the transfer hits a reciprocal lattice point:

k′ − k = G

A diffraction pattern is not a photograph of atoms — it is a photograph of the reciprocal lattice, one bright spot per G. Since |G| = 2π/d, Laue's condition and Bragg's law are the same physics in two languages.

The spot positions reveal the lattice; the spot intensities reveal the basis. Waves scattered by the different atoms within one unit cell interfere, summarized by the structure factor S(G) = ∑ fⱼ eiG·rⱼ over the basis atoms. Sometimes the interference is perfectly destructive: in a bcc crystal, reflections with Miller indices summing to an odd number vanish entirely. These systematic absences are fingerprints — the pattern of missing spots identifies the structure. Powder samples, with crystallites at every orientation, smear the spots into rings whose radii do the same job.

Did you know?

Lawrence Bragg was 25 when he won the 1915 Nobel Prize with his father — still the youngest science laureate ever. Forty years later, X-ray diffraction delivered its most famous result: Rosalind Franklin's "Photo 51," whose X-shaped pattern told Watson and Crick that DNA is a helix with 3.4 Å between base pairs. Reciprocal space read the code of life first.
