Solid State Physics

Vibrations & Phonons

Quantized lattice waves and the heat capacity of solids

Balls and Springs: The One-Dimensional Chain

Solid state physics' favorite toy model: an infinite chain of identical atoms, mass m, spacing a, joined by springs of stiffness κ. Displace atom n by uₙ and Newton's second law reads m·üₙ = κ(uₙ₊₁ + uₙ₋₁ − 2uₙ) — each atom pulled toward the average of its neighbors. Try a wave solution uₙ = A ei(kna − ωt) and the equation collapses to the chain's dispersion relation:

ω(k) = 2√(κ/m) · |sin(ka/2)|

This one formula repays study. At small k (wavelengths long compared with a), sin(ka/2) ≈ ka/2 and ω ≈ vk with v = a√(κ/m): the chain carries ordinary sound, with a speed set by stiffness over mass — the same √(spring/inertia) logic as every oscillator since Subject 8. The discreteness of the atoms is invisible to long waves, which is why continuum elasticity works so well.

Near the zone boundary k = π/a the curve flattens and the group velocity dω/dk drops to zero. There the wavelength is 2a: adjacent atoms swing in perfect antiphase, forming a standing wave that Bragg-reflects off the lattice itself and transports no energy. A discrete chain also has a maximum frequency, 2√(κ/m) — shake one end faster and the disturbance simply refuses to propagate.

The Brillouin zone strikes again

Shift k by 2π/a and uₙ = A e^(ikna) is unchanged at every atom — between the atoms there is nothing to wave. Wavevectors differing by a reciprocal lattice vector are physically identical, so every distinct vibration lives in the first Brillouin zone, −π/a < k ≤ π/a. A finite chain of N atoms holds exactly N allowed k-values in that zone: N atoms, N modes. Count conserved.

Did you know?

Plug in real numbers for copper — atomic mass, interatomic spring constants inferred from elastic moduli — and v = a√(κ/m) lands within a few percent of copper's measured sound speed of about 4700 m/s. Two atoms and a spring already know how fast sound travels in a metal.

Two Atoms per Cell: Acoustic Meets Optical

Real crystals often have more than one atom in the unit cell — NaCl has two, quartz has nine. The minimal upgrade is a chain with alternating masses m₁ and m₂. Solving it the same way yields not one dispersion curve but two branches for each k, and the split is one of the most important structural facts in the subject.

  • The acoustic branch behaves like before: ω → 0 as k → 0, with the two atoms in each cell moving together. Long-wavelength acoustic modes are just sound; they exist in every crystal because uniformly translating the whole lattice costs nothing.
  • The optical branch starts at a high frequency at k = 0 and stays high. Here the two atoms in each cell move against each other — the light atom one way, the heavy atom the other — while the cell's center of mass barely moves.

Between the branches at the zone boundary opens a frequency gap: a band of frequencies at which no wave can travel through the crystal at all. Waves in that band are exponentially damped — the crystal is a mechanical filter. (Hold that thought: in the electron chapter, the same mathematics will open gaps in energy rather than frequency, with civilization-scale consequences.)

Why "optical"?

In an ionic crystal like NaCl the two atoms carry opposite charges, so their out-of-phase dance is an oscillating electric dipole — which couples directly to light. Infrared radiation at the optical-mode frequency is absorbed ferociously; this is why salt is transparent in the visible but opaque in the infrared, and it is a major reason greenhouse gases and minerals absorb where they do.

The generalization is clean. In three dimensions with p atoms per unit cell there are 3p branches: 3 acoustic (one longitudinal, two transverse) and 3p − 3 optical. Diamond, with two carbons per cell, has three acoustic and three optical branches; its highest optical phonon, at about 40 THz, is the stiffest lattice vibration of any material — carbon springs are ferocious.

Did you know?

Neutron beams, with wavelengths matching a and energies matching ħω, can scatter off a single phonon and reveal the entire dispersion curve branch by branch. Measured curves for NaCl look strikingly like the two-mass chain's prediction — the toy model, essentially unmodified, is experimental reality.

Phonons: Particles of Sound

Everything so far was classical. Now recall Subject 26: each normal mode of the chain is mathematically a harmonic oscillator, and a quantum harmonic oscillator of frequency ω cannot hold arbitrary energy — only the rungs of a ladder:

E = ħω(n + ½),  n = 0, 1, 2, …

Quantizing every mode of the lattice therefore chops vibrational energy into lumps of ħω. Each lump behaves like a particle: it carries energy ħω, crystal momentum ħk, and can be created, destroyed, and scattered. We call it a phonon — a particle of sound, the exact acoustic analogue of the photon. A hot crystal is a box full of phonon gas.

Because any number of phonons can pile into the same mode, phonons are bosons, and in thermal equilibrium the average number in a mode of frequency ω follows the Bose–Einstein distribution:

⟨n⟩ = 1/(eħω/kBT − 1)

Read its two limits — they run the next section. When kBT ≫ ħω, ⟨n⟩ ≈ kBT/ħω: the mode holds energy kBT, the classical equipartition answer, and quantum mechanics hides. When kBT ≪ ħω, ⟨n⟩ ≈ e−ħω/kBT: the mode is frozen out, too expensive to excite even once, and contributes essentially nothing to the thermal energy.

Analogy: The Vending Machine

A mode of frequency ω is a vending machine whose cheapest item costs ħω, and temperature is your pocket money kBT. Rich compared to the price, you buy freely and never notice items have discrete prices — that is the classical limit. Poor compared to the price, you almost never buy at all. There is no option to purchase half an item: that is quantization, and it is the entire resolution of the heat-capacity crisis coming next.

Phonons also do real work in the lab and in your laptop. They are the dominant carriers of heat in insulators, they scatter electrons (a major source of electrical resistance, as chapter three will show), and — a promissory note for the final chapter — exchanging phonons is what glues electrons into the Cooper pairs behind superconductivity.

Einstein vs Debye: The Heat-Capacity Showdown

Here was the crisis. Classical equipartition assigns each atom kBT per quadratic degree of freedom — three kinetic plus three potential — predicting a molar heat capacity of 3R ≈ 25 J/(mol·K) for every solid at every temperature. This Dulong–Petit law works beautifully at room temperature for lead and copper… and fails for diamond, and fails for everything when cooled: measured heat capacities plunge toward zero as T → 0. Classical physics has no explanation whatsoever.

Einstein, 1907: model every atom as an independent quantum oscillator with one shared frequency ωE. His vending-machine logic gives Dulong–Petit at high temperature — and at low temperature the modes freeze out, so the heat capacity falls. One fitted frequency per solid reproduced the data remarkably well, and the physics community's resistance to quantum theory cracked: heat capacities, not light, were quantization's first mass conversion. Diamond stopped being an anomaly — its bonds are so stiff that ħωE exceeds kBT even at room temperature; diamond at 300 K is already "cold."

But precision data disagreed at the bottom end: Einstein's exponential freeze-out e−ħωE/kBT dives too fast. Experiment showed a gentler power law: C ∝ T³.

Debye, 1912: atoms are not independent — they are coupled, and the chain taught us the modes are waves with ω = vk down to arbitrarily low frequency. However cold the crystal, there are always long-wavelength acoustic phonons cheap enough to excite. Counting sound modes up to a cutoff (the Debye temperature ΘD, chosen so the total is 3N modes) gives Dulong–Petit at high T and, at low T,

C = (12π⁴/5) N kB (T/ΘD)³

— the T³ law, in superb agreement with experiment for essentially all insulating solids.

Why Debye wins at low T

Einstein's single frequency makes the coldest excitation cost fixed money ħωE, so activity shuts down exponentially. Debye's acoustic spectrum offers excitations at every price down to zero; cooling merely shrinks the affordable slice of k-space, and counting modes with ħω < kBT in three dimensions gives energy ∝ T⁴, hence C ∝ T³. The same photon counting in a box yields blackbody radiation's Stefan–Boltzmann T⁴ law — Debye's solid is a blackbody made of sound.

Did you know?

ΘD ranges from 105 K for soft, heavy lead to about 2200 K for diamond. Above roughly ΘD a solid behaves classically; below, quantum mechanics runs its thermodynamics. By that standard, lead at room temperature is a classical solid — and diamond is deeply quantum.
